What the studies found about sounding out words

The short answer

Does teaching a child to sound out words really help them read?

Yes, according to two large reviews. An expert panel found strong evidence for teaching children to blend letter sounds into words, to learn common spelling patterns and to recognize common words. The earlier National Reading Panel reached the same conclusion about systematic phonics.

What the research found

123456
  • 6 × Strong evidence

  1. Eighteen studies of teaching children to decode words, analyze word parts and write words met the review's standards. Thirteen of them found positive effects on reading words, on spelling them, or on both.

    Strong evidenceSource 1, Recommendation 3, summary of evidence, page 23

  2. Eleven of those studies found positive effects on reading words, and four found positive effects on spelling.

    Strong evidenceSource 1, Recommendation 3, summary of evidence, page 23

  3. The children in the studies were in kindergarten through third grade. Eight studies looked at children at risk for reading difficulties, and others included children of all ability levels.

    Strong evidenceSource 1, Recommendation 3, summary of evidence, page 23

  4. The panel says this teaching can begin once a child knows a few consonants and vowels. Its first step is to teach children to blend letter sounds from left to right within a word.

    Strong evidenceSource 1, Recommendation 3, pages 22 to 23

  5. The panel's other steps are: teach common spelling patterns, teach common word parts, have children read decodable words alone and in text, teach common words so they are recognized quickly, and introduce the few words a text needs that cannot yet be sounded out.

    Strong evidenceSource 1, Recommendation 3, how to carry out the recommendation

  6. The National Reading Panel reported that its analysis showed systematic phonics instruction helps children succeed in learning to read.

    Strong evidenceSource 2, findings on phonics instruction

What this means at home

  • Teach a few letter sounds, then start blending them into short words right away. Do not wait for the whole alphabet.
  • Blend from left to right, the same way every time.
  • Give your child words and short sentences made only of sounds they have been taught.
  • If your child knows the sounds but cannot put them together, the free blending check points to the likely reason.

What the research does not tell us

  • Thirteen of the eighteen studies found positive effects. This page does not report what the other five found, because the summary we read does not say.
  • All of the studies took place in schools. None tested parents teaching at home.
  • No study that met the standards measured what children learned about word parts and their meanings.
  • A review of many children cannot say how quickly any one child will learn.

How we write these pages

  1. We read the source itself, not a news story about it.
  2. Every finding on a page names its source and where in the source it is found, so you can check it.
  3. We say how strong the evidence is, in the words of the people who reviewed it.
  4. We say what the research does not tell us. Most studies are done in schools, and no study can say what one child needs.
  5. If we get something wrong, tell us at hello@sounditoutreading.com and we will correct the page.
